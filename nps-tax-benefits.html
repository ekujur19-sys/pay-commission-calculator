NPS tax benefits for Central Government employees

Your NPS contributions save tax in three different ways, and only one of them survives in the new tax regime. Here is what you can claim, with examples.

Last updated October 2026 · by NE Media

New section numbers. From 1 April 2026 the Income-tax Act, 2025 replaced the 1961 Act, and the NPS deductions moved from section 80CCD to the new deduction sections (section 124 for NPS, section 123 for the old 80C list). The rules and limits below did not change. Most people and many offices still use the old names, so we give both.

Who contributes what

  • You: 10% of basic pay plus DA, deducted from your salary every month.
  • The Government: 14% of basic pay plus DA, paid into your account on top of your salary.
  • Employees who chose the Unified Pension Scheme (UPS) also contribute 10%; the Government's share is 10% to the employee's account plus 8.5% to a common pool. The Government has extended the NPS tax treatment to UPS contributions.

Example: Level 7, basic pay ₹47,600, DA 60%. Basic plus DA is ₹76,160 a month, ₹9,13,920 a year.

How the Government's 14% works

The Government's contribution is first added to your salary income, then deducted in full. The net effect is that it is never taxed while it goes in, in either regime. In the example above, ₹1,27,949 a year goes into your NPS account tax-free.

How to use the extra ₹50,000

Your compulsory 10% (₹91,392 in the example) normally sits inside the ₹1.5 lakh limit along with PPF, insurance and other savings. You can claim the separate ₹50,000 in two ways:

  • If your other savings already use up the ₹1.5 lakh, claim up to ₹50,000 of your compulsory NPS contribution under the extra deduction instead.
  • Or make a voluntary contribution to your NPS Tier I account (through your PRAN online) and claim that.

At the 20% slab, the extra ₹50,000 saves ₹10,400 in tax (with 4% cess); at the 30% slab, ₹15,600. These deductions only count in the old regime.

Tier II tax saver

Central Government employees can also invest in the Tier II Tax Saver Scheme, which has a three-year lock-in. It counts towards the ₹1.5 lakh limit in the old regime.

Old regime or new regime?

In the new regime you keep only the 14% Government contribution deduction, but slabs are lower and income up to ₹12 lakh is tax-free (₹12.75 lakh for salaried employees after the standard deduction). Many employees up to about Level 7 or 8 (depending on their cell and city) pay no income tax at all in the new regime. Those with large old-regime deductions (HRA, home loan interest, full ₹1.5 lakh plus ₹50,000) can still do better in the old regime. Compare both with your own figures in the salary calculator, and read old vs new tax regime.

Tax when you take the money out

  • At retirement: up to 60% of the corpus can be taken as a lump sum, tax-free. The rest buys an annuity; the annuity (pension) you get each month is taxed as income.
  • Partial withdrawal while in service: up to 25% of your own contributions, for purposes such as children's education or marriage, buying a house or medical treatment, after three years. It is tax-free.
  • UPS: the assured payout is taxed like a pension; the lump sum at retirement is tax-free in the same way as the NPS lump sum.

See the NPS vs UPS calculator to estimate your corpus and pension.

Common mistakes

  • Counting the 10% twice. The same rupee cannot be claimed under both the ₹1.5 lakh limit and the extra ₹50,000.
  • Forgetting to declare. Tell your office's pay section which regime you want and which deductions you claim at the start of the year, so the right TDS is deducted each month.
  • Ignoring the arrears year. When 8th CPC arrears are paid, your NPS deduction on the arrears also rises. See arrears and tax relief.

Sources

Tax rules change with each Budget; check before you file.
